What was actually wrong

The project began with a model scoring AUC 0.32, worse than a coin flip, that found 0 of 392 seizures. The stored results claimed 99% accuracy.

The cause was one regular expression. CHB-MIT annotation files write multi-seizure recordings as Seizure 1 Start Time: 1724 seconds, and the parser took the first number it found:

match = re.search(r'(\d+)', line)   # captures "1", the seizure index
                                    # not 1724, the timestamp

Every seizure was recorded as starting and ending at second 1, so no samples were ever labelled. Eighteen of 24 patients lost their entire ground truth. Files with a single unnumbered seizure survived, which is why six patients looked fine and hid the problem.

Repairing the labels took the dataset from 564 to 5,408 seizure segments, and from 6 to 24 patients with usable annotations. That single fix moved AUC from 0.32 to 0.78, more than every subsequent experiment combined.

Everything in the middle group landed on AUC 0.76-0.79 regardless of setting. That plateau is a property of this architecture reading raw waveforms, not of any hyperparameter, and not of the class imbalance that the project originally set out to solve.

On the imbalance. It was never the real problem. The data appeared six times more imbalanced than it was, because the bug had erased most positive labels. The true rate is 1.363%, which is simply what scalp EEG is. Every method aimed at rebalancing it, synthetic data included, changed nothing.

Four things that fooled the measurements

The most transferable findings here are about measurement, not modelling. Each of these produced a confidently wrong number before it was caught.

Counting alarm runs is gameable

False alarms were counted as contiguous runs of positive predictions. A threshold low enough to flag everything merges into one run per recording, which overlaps a real seizure and therefore books almost no false alarms, scoring ~0 FA/h at 100% sensitivity while being useless. This appeared four separate times in different scripts before being fixed with a flagged-time bound in all of them.

AUC cannot see what breaks end-to-end

Cases chb01 and chb21 are the same child recorded 18 months apart, and our splits put her on both sides. Fixing it moved AUC by 0.013, and event sensitivity by 0.084. Judged on AUC alone the leak looked harmless; it was not.

Patient grouping moves the headline by +/-8 points

One reshuffle of which patients sit in which fold swung individual results violently, CHB06 from 33% to 100%, CHB14 from 43% to 14%, CHB12 from 95% to 64%. Same data, same model. Any comparison finer than about +/-0.08 is noise at 3 folds, which retroactively invalidates several small differences chased during this work.

The scorer changes the answer by 3.4x

Identical predictions score 14.9 FA/h under a strict rule and 4.4 FA/h under SzCORE event rules (90 s merging, 30/60 s tolerance). Sensitivity moves too, 78% to 83%. Published false-alarm rates are not comparable unless the scorer is named.

The result sits inside the honest published range. The Gotman line is the useful calibration: high sensitivity is cheap, a 1982 rule-based detector reaches 91% if you accept enough false alarms. Suppressing false alarms is the entire difficulty, and it remains unsolved here.

The much lower challenge figure is not a weaker system. It was measured across hospitals on data no entrant had seen, and 23% of those patients were missed by every top-five algorithm. Single-institution numbers, including ours, should be read as optimistic.

Limitations

  • Not clinically usable. Continuous monitoring wants under 1 false alarm per hour; this is at 4.4.
  • Personalisation is part of the method. 5 of 24 patients were fine-tuned on one of their own earlier seizures, evaluated only on later ones. For a patient with no recorded seizure, the base model applies and performance is lower.
  • One hospital, 24 patients, all paediatric. No cross-institution validation.
  • Three folds is too few given the +/-8-point grouping variance. Leave-one-patient-out would be needed to resolve anything smaller.
  • Bimodal by patient. Roughly a third of patients reach 100% sensitivity at under ~1 FA/h; a handful produce most of the false alarms. The average describes almost nobody.

What would actually move it

Data volume and hyperparameters are exhausted, that is what the nine null and negative results establish. Three directions remain, in descending confidence:

  • Longer temporal context. Classifying 4-second windows independently discards the structure that distinguishes a seizure from a transient artifact. Systems using 60-80 s of context report markedly lower false-alarm rates, and this is the change most directly aimed at the one unsolved problem.
  • More patients, not more data. 24 people is few for learning what seizures look like in general, and the failures are patient-specific rather than uniform.
  • A pre-trained EEG backbone. Verified as a drop-in candidate: CBraMod (4.9M parameters, 9,000 hours of pretraining) loads cleanly and accepts this montage natively. Independent benchmarks are lukewarm, on several seizure tasks these models match their own random initialisation, and no foundation model has ever published an event-level CHB-MIT result. That gap is worth filling, but it needs leave-one-patient-out evaluation to be resolvable above the noise.
